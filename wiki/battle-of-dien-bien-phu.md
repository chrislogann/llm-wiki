# [[battle-of-dien-bien-phu|Battle of Dien Bien Phu]]

**Summary**: The decisive 1954 siege of the [[first-indochina-war|First Indochina War]], in which Viet Minh forces under General [[vo-nguyen-giap|Vo Nguyen Giap]] surrounded and destroyed a heavily fortified French garrison, ending French colonial rule in Indochina.

**Sources**: `Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-08-11

---

The **[[battle-of-dien-bien-phu|Battle of Dien Bien Phu]]** (March 13 – May 7, 1954) was the climactic engagement of the **[[first-indochina-war|First Indochina War]]** (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md). It took place in a remote valley in northwestern Vietnam near the Laotian border (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md).

## French Strategy
French commander General Henri Navarre conceived the "Navarre Plan" to break the military stalemate (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md). The French established an air-head and a cluster of strongpoints in the valley of Dien Bien Phu, garrisoned by over 13,000 elite troops under Colonel Christian de Castries (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md). 

Navarre calculated that the Viet Minh would be unable to supply a large force in the remote jungle mountains or deploy heavy artillery on the surrounding ridges, allowing French airpower and artillery to easily destroy any attackers (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md).

## [[vo-nguyen-giap|Giap]]'s Mobilization and Tactics
Viet Minh General **[[vo-nguyen-giap|Vo Nguyen Giap]]** accepted the challenge (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md). He launched a massive logistical operation, mobilizing over 250,000 peasant porters (known as the "steel horses" because they pushed heavily laden bicycles) to transport supplies and disassembled Chinese-supplied heavy artillery over hundreds of miles of jungle tracks (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md). 

[[vo-nguyen-giap|Giap]]'s forces dug miles of tunnels and trenches, placing their artillery inside camouflaged bunkers directly overlooking the French airstrip and fortifications (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md).

## The Siege
The battle began on March 13, 1954, with a surprise Viet Minh bombardment that quickly destroyed the French airstrip and silenced French counter-battery fire (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md). Realizing his calculations had failed, the French artillery commander, Colonel Charles Piroth, committed suicide in his bunker (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md). 

With the airstrip unusable, the French could only be supplied by parachute drops, which were frequently intercepted by Viet Minh anti-aircraft fire (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md). [[vo-nguyen-giap|Giap]]'s infantry systematically squeezed the French perimeter, launching human-wave assaults to capture the outer strongpoints (named Beatrice, Gabrielle, Anne-Marie, and Eliane) (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md). 

Despite desperate French resistance and appeals for US airstrikes (Operation Vulture, which President Eisenhower declined), the garrison fell on May 7, 1954 (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md).

## Geopolitical Impact
The French suffered over 7,000 casualties and 11,000 captured, while the Viet Minh suffered an estimated 20,000 casualties (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md). The defeat coincided with the opening of the Geneva Conference, forcing the French government to negotiate a withdrawal from Indochina and ending nearly a century of French colonial presence in Southeast Asia (source: Vietnam,_a_history_9780670746040.md).
