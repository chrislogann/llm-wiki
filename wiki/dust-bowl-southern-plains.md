# Dust Bowl: The Southern Plains in the 1930s

**Summary**: Donald Worster's seminal environmental history analyzing the 1930s Dust Bowl as a crisis of capitalism and one of the worst ecological blunders in human history.

**Sources**: [Dust_Bowl_-_the_southern_Plains_in_the_1930s_9780195174885.md]

**Last updated**: 2026-08-15

---

In *Dust Bowl: The Southern Plains in the 1930s*, environmental historian [[donald-worster|Donald Worster]] argues that the [[dust-bowl|Dust Bowl]] was not a natural disaster or an accident of weather, but the direct and inevitable consequence of American capitalist culture applied to a volatile, marginal ecosystem (source: Dust_Bowl_-_the_southern_Plains_in_the_1930s_9780195174885.md).

## Capitalism and the Soil
Worster identifies three core maxims of the capitalist ethos that drove the environmental destruction (source: Dust_Bowl_-_the_southern_Plains_in_the_1930s_9780195174885.md):
1. **Nature as Capital**: The natural world is desanctified and viewed purely as economic assets to be converted into commodities and profit.
2. **Right to Maximize**: Individuals and businesses have an unlimited right, and even an obligation, to maximize their extraction of wealth from the land.
3. **Institutional Support**: The social and political order is designed to support individual accumulation while absorbing or ignoring the environmental costs.

This expansionary drive led to the rapid mechanization and plow-up of the Southern Plains during the 1920s, removing the native grasses that held the soil together (source: Dust_Bowl_-_the_southern_Plains_in_the_1930s_9780195174885.md). When severe drought struck in the 1930s, the soil simply blew away.

## Economic and Ecological Crisis
Worster links the [[dust-bowl|Dust Bowl]] directly to the [[great-depression|Great Depression]] (source: Dust_Bowl_-_the_southern_Plains_in_the_1930s_9780195174885.md). Both crises occurred simultaneously and stemmed from the same systemic vulnerabilities: Wall Street ignored economic limits and overextended credit, while plains farmers ignored environmental limits and overextended agriculture (source: Dust_Bowl_-_the_southern_Plains_in_the_1930s_9780195174885.md).

## Local Case Studies
The book examines the localized human and ecological impacts of the disaster through two distinct case studies (source: Dust_Bowl_-_the_southern_Plains_in_the_1930s_9780195174885.md):
- **[[cimarron-county|Cimarron County, Oklahoma]]**: Positioned at the very heart of the blow area, representing a frontier in ruins with severe wind erosion and a desperate cattle crisis where herds were forced to eat tumbleweeds.
- **[[haskell-county|Haskell County, Kansas]]**: A flat wheat-farming landscape where farmers behaved as gamblers, experiencing extreme economic instability but remaining heavily dependent on the New Deal welfare state to survive.
