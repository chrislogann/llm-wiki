# Soviet-Afghan War

**Summary**: The decade-long military conflict (1979–1989) in which the Soviet 40th Army invaded and occupied Afghanistan to prop up a failing communist client regime, resulting in strategic defeat, massive economic exhaustion, and political delegitimization that accelerated the collapse of the Soviet Union.

**Sources**: `The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-08-11

---

The Soviet-Afghan War began on December 25, 1979, when the Soviet Politburo (driven primarily by KGB Chairman [[yuri-andropov|Yuri Andropov]], Defense Minister Dmitry Ustinov, and Foreign Minister Andrei Gromyko) ordered the 40th Army to invade the Democratic Republic of Afghanistan (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md). The intervention was intended as a swift stabilization operation to prevent the overthrow of the pro-Soviet People's Democratic Party of Afghanistan (PDPA) regime, but deteriorated into an intractable ten-year guerrilla war against nationwide Islamic *mujahideen* resistance (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).

## Phases of the Conflict

1. **Invasion and Leadership Liquidation (December 1979)**:
   - On December 27, 1979, Soviet KGB special forces (*Spetsnaz* / Zenith and Alfa groups) stormed the Tajbeg Palace in Kabul (Operation Storm-333), assassinating PDPA President Hafizullah Amin, whom Moscow deemed erratic and untrustworthy (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).
   - Moscow installed Babrak Karmal (leader of the rival Parcham faction) as head of state and poured over 100,000 Soviet troops into Afghan cities and garrisons (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).

2. **Guerrilla Warfare and Stagnation (1980–1986)**:
   - The Soviet armed forces controlled the major cities and highways but proved unable to pacify the mountainous rural regions (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).
   - The *mujahideen* received extensive international support, training, and financing from the United States (Operation Cyclone), Pakistan, Saudi Arabia, and China (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).
   - The introduction of shoulder-fired Stinger anti-aircraft missiles in 1986 neutralized Soviet helicopter gunship (*Mil Mi-24 Hind*) superiority, radically shifting tactical dynamics in favor of the insurgents (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).

3. **Withdrawal and Imperial Repercussions (1986–1989)**:
   - Recognizing the war as an open "bleeding wound" (*krovotochashchaya rana*), Mikhail Gorbachev replaced Babrak Karmal with Mohammad Najibullah and initiated withdrawal plans (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).
   - Under the [[geneva-accords-1954|Geneva Accords]] of April 1988, Soviet troops began their phased departure, with the final units crossing the Friendship Bridge back into Soviet territory on February 15, 1989 (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).

## Impact on the Collapse of the Soviet Empire

The Afghan defeat was a primary domestic and geopolitical factor in the destruction of the USSR (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md):
- **Military Delegitimization**: It shattered the myth of the Red Army's invincibility, generating deep cynicism among conscripts and the Soviet public (the "Afghan Syndrome") (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).
- **Catalyst for Glasnost**: Public anger over casualties and government secrecy forced the Soviet leadership to allow open criticism in the media, accelerating the erosion of state information control (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).
- **Economic Strain**: The astronomical costs of maintaining the expeditionary army hastened Soviet financial and supply-chain collapse (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).
