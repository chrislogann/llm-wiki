# Dispersion of the Topeka Legislature (1856)

**Summary**: The forced dissolution of the Free-State Topeka Legislature by U.S. Army Colonel Edwin Vose Sumner on July 4, 1856, acting under orders from U.S. President Franklin Pierce.

**Sources**: [thirty-years-in-topeka.md]

**Last updated**: 2026-08-15

---

The dispersion of the Free-State Topeka Legislature on July 4, 1856, was one of the most critical political confrontations of the Territorial Kansas era (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md). It demonstrated the federal government's willingness to use military force to suppress the anti-slavery movement in Kansas (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md).

## The Military Approach
As the Free-State legislature prepared to convene in Topeka on July 4, a young courier warned the town that Colonel Edwin Vose Sumner and U.S. Army dragoons were approaching in battle array (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md). Sumner rode into Kansas Avenue with nearly 200 dragoons and three artillery pieces, pointing the cannon directly down the street with slow matches burning (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md). 

Despite the military presence, the crowds stood firm, and local militias held their positions (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md).

## Confrontation in the Legislative Halls
Colonel Sumner dismounted and entered the House of Representatives, where members were gathered (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md). Addresssing the assembly, Sumner stated:
> *"Gentlemen, I am called upon this day to do the most painful duty of my life. Under the authority of the President's proclamation, I am here to disperse the Legislature... I repeat that this is the most painful duty of my life."* (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md)

When asked if the members were being driven out at the point of the bayonet, Sumner affirmed he would use whatever forces were at his command to carry out his orders, forcing the House representatives to disperse (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md).

## The Senate and Marshal Donaldson
Sumner then entered the Senate Chamber upstairs (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md). The Senators stood in a silent circle and refused to respond (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md). When Sumner asked if they considered themselves dispersed, the Senate President replied that they could not make any reply because the Senate was not formally in session (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md). 

At that point, U.S. Marshal Donaldson stepped forward and threatened to arrest every Senator who did not pledge never to meet again (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md). The Senators ignored Donaldson's threats and acknowledged Sumner's order, stating they would yield only to the authority of the United States troops (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md).

## Aftermath and Legacy
Sumner left the hall and assured the citizens that they had the right to remain assembled in a peaceful convention, as his orders were strictly limited to dispersing the formal legislature (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md). The citizens cheered for Sumner, John C. Fremont, and the Topeka Constitution (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md). 

However, because Sumner had expressed personal regret and political neutrality during his address to the legislature, U.S. Secretary of War Jefferson Davis immediately superseded Sumner in his military command (source: thirty-years-in-topeka.md).
