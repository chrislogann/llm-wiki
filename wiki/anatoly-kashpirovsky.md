# Anatoly Kashpirovsky

**Summary**: A self-styled Soviet-era TV psychic healer, psychologist, and celebrity who gained massive popularity during the collapse of the Soviet Union and attempted comebacks during subsequent national and global crises.

**Sources**: `As COVID-19 Hits Russia, A Self-Styled Psychic Healer And Soviet-Era Icon Returns.md`, `5-Voloshyna.md`, `A “Common Madness” TV Psychics and Hypnosis in the Soviet Union.md`, `Soviet Union’s mass delusion Psychic healer who ‘cured’ millions through TV dies.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-08-18

---

Anatoly Kashpirovsky is a classically trained psychiatrist who graduated from the Vinnytsya Medical Institute and worked in a psychiatric hospital for 25 years before rising to national celebrity in the Soviet Union in the late 1980s (source: 5-Voloshyna.md). While he insists that his methods are strictly medical and scientific, his rise to fame and subsequent resurgence occurred during periods of intense social and political crisis, causing laypeople to treat him as a faith healer and messianic figure (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).

## The "Biological Self-Regulatory" Theory and Sports Training
Kashpirovsky explicitly rejects labels such as "hypnosis," "magic," "charlatan," or "folk healer" (source: 5-Voloshyna.md). Instead, he explains his work through a theory of biological self-regulation:
* **Subconscious Commands**: He claims the human body genetically encodes all the chemical elements required to cure itself of any illness (source: 5-Voloshyna.md). His therapy provides subconscious commands that trigger this self-regulatory mechanism (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **Athletic Suggestion**: In the 1970s and 1980s, Kashpirovsky applied these hypnotherapy and auto-suggestion techniques to sports. He worked closely with the Soviet weightlifting team that won ten golds at the 1987 World Championships, training athletes in "emotional-volitional training" to believe in their bodies' physiological capabilities (source: A “Common Madness” TV Psychics and Hypnosis in the Soviet Union.md).
* **O'Connor's Vitalism**: Anthropologists identify this model as a form of vitalism—the belief that the body contains a self-promoting "life force" essential to wellness (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **Dominance and Backlash**: Kashpirovsky maintains a highly dominant position over his patients. He is extremely sensitive to criticism and has threatened critics with "psychological backlash" (e.g., warning that he could maliciously alter the shape of their noses or worsen skin conditions) (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).

## Late-Soviet Era Celebrity and Gosteleradio Broadcasts
During the final chaotic years of the USSR (marked by the Chernobyl disaster, Glasnost, and economic decline), Kashpirovsky's television broadcasts offered a liminal space of hope, ranking him second in popularity only to Boris Yeltsin (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **Mass Popularity**: A 1990 All-Union Centre for Public Opinion Research (VTsIOM) survey reported that 57% of viewers put aside all activities to watch his health séances, and streets were reported empty during broadcasts (source: A “Common Madness” TV Psychics and Hypnosis in the Soviet Union.md).
* **1988 Kyiv-Moscow Telebridge**: Guided a patient through televised breast cancer tumor removal surgery without anesthesia. While the patient sang on camera, she later admitted she was in agony and only pretended to be fine due to televised pressure, and that doctors had secretly administered local anesthesia (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **1989 Kyiv-Tbilisi Telebridge**: Guided two simultaneous hernia surgeries without chemical anesthesia for patients allergic to anesthetic drugs (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **Televised Jars**: Viewers routinely placed jars of water near their TV screens during his broadcasts, hoping to "charge" the water in a way that mimicked Orthodox Christian water-blessing rituals (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **Feud with Allan Chumak**: Kashpirovsky engaged in a bitter, decades-long feud with his chief competitor [[allan-chumak]] (who silently "charged" water on TV) over establishing who had appeared on television first (source: Soviet Union’s mass delusion Psychic healer who ‘cured’ millions through TV dies.md).

## Medical Backlash and 1990 Cancellation
By late 1989, Kashpirovsky's reputation began unravelling due to health complaints:
* **Hospital Admissions**: *Pravda* reported a sharp spike in hospital admissions for cardiovascular complaints, hypertension, and mental crises immediately following séances (source: A “Common Madness” TV Psychics and Hypnosis in the Soviet Union.md).
* **Diabetic Coma Case**: The Ministry of Health expressed frustration with Kashpirovsky's refusal to cooperate with research, citing the case of a 30-year-old diabetic patient who suffered psychosis and hallucinations after watching his broadcast, ending up in an artificial coma (source: A “Common Madness” TV Psychics and Hypnosis in the Soviet Union.md).
* **Orthodox Backlash and Cancellation**: Facing warnings from established medical professionals and intense lobbying from the Orthodox Church, Gosteleradio canceled his show in early 1990 (source: A “Common Madness” TV Psychics and Hypnosis in the Soviet Union.md).

## COVID-19 Resurgence on YouTube
After years of living in the United States and hosting low-profile local sessions in Brighton Beach, New York (source: 5-Voloshyna.md), Kashpirovsky transitioned to YouTube during the 2020–2021 COVID-19 pandemic:
* **Online Health Sessions**: Broadcasting live every two to three days, his streams drew hundreds of thousands of views from people seeking symptom relief (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **Patient Testimonials**: Comments on his YouTube channel claimed diverse cures, including improvements in lung capacity (holding breath from 15 to 70 seconds), return of smell and taste, normalized blood pressure, and aesthetic enhancements (disappearing scars, plumper lips) (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **Vaccine Skepticism**: He used his platform to criticize the Russian Sputnik V vaccine, claiming the vaccine caused the death of Yalta Mayor Ivan Imgrunt (which health officials denied), and accused Western/American vaccines of being part of a plot to destroy Russia (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).

His broad societal influence during times of crisis has drawn frequent comparisons to the 20th-century mystic Rasputin, who similarly leveraged claims of faith healing over the Imperial family during the collapse of the Russian Empire (source: As COVID-19 Hits Russia, A Self-Styled Psychic Healer And Soviet-Era Icon Returns.md).
