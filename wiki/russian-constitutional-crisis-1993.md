# 1993 Russian Constitutional Crisis

**Summary**: The violent political stand-off between President Boris Yeltsin and parliament in September-October 1993, resulting in the shelling of the White House and consolidation of executive power.

**Sources**: `The_New_Tsar_-_the_rise_and_reign_of_Vladimir_Putin_9780307961617.md`, `The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-08-16

---

The 1993 Russian Constitutional Crisis (September 21 – October 4, 1993) was a violent political stand-off between President Boris Yeltsin and the Russian Parliament (the Supreme Soviet and the Congress of People's Deputies). It resulted in over 100 deaths, the armed shelling of the parliament building, and the consolidation of near-absolute executive authority under the Russian presidency.

## Stalemate and Preemptive Actions
By late 1993, the Russian government was gridlocked. Parliament speaker Ruslan Khasbulatov and Vice President Aleksandr Rutskoy led opposition deputies in systematically blocking Yeltsin's market reforms and constitutional amendments (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).

On September 21, 1993, Yeltsin issued a decree titled *"On stage-by-stage constitutional reform in the Russian Federation,"* suspending the parliament's legislative powers and scheduling elections for a new bicameral Federal Assembly. Forewarned of the decree by a leak, Khasbulatov and Rutskoy began stockpiling arms inside the parliament building (the White House) (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).

## Escalation and Impeachment
Following the decree, the Russian Constitutional Court ruled Yeltsin's actions unconstitutional. Parliament voted to impeach Yeltsin and swore in Rutskoy as the rival President. Yeltsin responded by cutting off telephone, power, and water lines to the White House and deploying police cordons (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).

## Moscow Violence and Ostankino Seizure
On October 3, armed pro-parliament protesters broke the police lines. From a White House balcony, Rutskoy called for a popular uprising. Protesters seized the Moscow mayor's office and stormed the Ostankino television tower, briefly knocking state TV off the air. The Russian military initially hesitated to interfere, but Defense Minister Pavel Grachev agreed to deploy forces once Yeltsin signed a formal, written order authorizing the use of tanks in Moscow (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).

## Shelling of the White House
At 7:00 AM on October 4, Russian tanks encircled the White House and began firing shells into the upper floors, setting the building on fire in full view of television cameras. By early evening, Khasbulatov, Rutskoy, and other leaders surrendered. They were imprisoned in Lefortovo Prison but later amnestied in February 1994 (source: The_rise_and_fall_of_the_Soviet_Empire_9780761520573.md).

## St. Petersburg Response
In St. Petersburg, Mayor [[anatoly-sobchak|Anatoly Sobchak]] backed Yeltsin, going on television to appeal for calm and locking his vice mayor, Vyacheslav Shcherbakov, out of office for supporting the parliament (source: The_New_Tsar_-_the_rise_and_reign_of_Vladimir_Putin_9780307961617.md).

His deputy, [[vladimir-putin|Vladimir Putin]], and security chief Viktor Cherkesov deployed special forces to defend the city's television center. Sobchak also sent a squad of St. Petersburg special forces to Moscow to assist Yeltsin during the height of the standoff (source: The_New_Tsar_-_the_rise_and_reign_of_Vladimir_Putin_9780307961617.md).
