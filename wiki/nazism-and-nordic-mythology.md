# Nazism and Nordic Mythology

**Summary**: A summary of Aidan Neher's Carleton College essay detailing how the Nazi regime, particularly the SS under Heinrich Himmler, misappropriated Norse myths, symbols, and history to create a "religion of blood" and recruit Scandinavian volunteers.

**Sources**: `Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-08-04

---

Adolf Hitler and the Nazi party established a totalitarian regime (the Third Reich) following the economic collapse of the Weimar Republic after World War I (source: Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md). To unify Germanic and "Aryan" peoples under a common past, the Nazis attempted to construct a neo-pagan "religion of the blood" (source: Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md).

## Major Themes of Misappropriation

The essay categorizes the misappropriation of Norse lore by Nazi propaganda into four distinct themes:

1. **The SS as Viking Reincarnates**: Visually pairing members of the [[heinrich-himmler|SS]] alongside noble-looking Vikings to portray them as elite, honorable, and predestined warriors (source: Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md). This was heavily used for recruiting Norwegian volunteers (source: Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md).
2. **Germanic History and Fate**: Using images of Viking ships to liken WWII Nazi expansion (specifically the war against Bolshevism to the East) to historical [[viking-expansion|Viking expansion]], justifying it as predestined (source: Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md).
3. **Viking Myths and Symbols**: Direct appropriation of Norse legends, such as using the wolf [[fenrir]] to symbolize the Soviet threat, or drawing on the god-fueled frenzy of [[berserks]] to frame participation in the war effort as righteous (source: Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md).
4. **Men of the North**: Using cold, icy mountain backgrounds and Norse-inspired imagery to assert environmental and climatic unity among Norway, Nazi Germany, and Finland, culminating in a shared battle akin to [[ragnarok]] (source: Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md).

The co-optation of ancient symbols like the [[swastika]] and [[sig-rune]] has led to their legal ban in modern Germany and fostered a social stigma around Norse studies (source: Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md). This stigma has contributed to public ignorance and the propagation of popular myths about Viking culture (source: Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md).

## Parallel Exploitation of Occultism
The regime's utilization of Norse mythology was part of a broader mobilization of irrationalism and the occult, detailed in [[occultism-in-nazi-germany]] (source: Nazism and Nordic Mythology.md). While Himmler and Willigut worked to construct an elite "religion of blood" at the state level, the Nazi propaganda machine concurrently exploited popular superstitions, astrologers, and clairvoyants like [[erik-jan-hanussen]] to manipulate public consciousness and consolidate control over the German citizenry (source: Propaganda_als_Waffe_9783752659368.md).
