# Grammatical Fiction

**Summary**: The "grammatical fiction" is Nicolas Rubashov's term for the first-person singular ("I"), representing his long-suppressed conscience and the moral guilt over sacrificed comrades.

**Sources**: `Darkness_at_Noon_9780226449616.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-08-04

---

In *Darkness at Noon*, the "grammatical fiction" represents the emergence of the first-person singular ("I") within a political system that only recognizes the collective "we" (source: Darkness_at_Noon_9780226449616.md). For decades, the Party trained its members to reject personal feeling and conscience as bourgeois sentimentality, but in the isolation of cell No. 404, Rubashov finds his conscience speaking as an independent "silent partner" (source: Darkness_at_Noon_9780226449616.md).

## The Case of Arlova

The primary source of Rubashov's guilt is Arlova, his former secretary and lover:
- **Arlova's Trial**: When Arlova was accused of oppositional connections, she repeatedly called upon Rubashov to testify to her loyalty (source: Darkness_at_Noon_9780226449616.md).
- **The Sacrifice**: Rather than defending her, Rubashov remained silent and publicly disavowed her to protect his own position and continue his work for the Party (source: Darkness_at_Noon_9780226449616.md). This lie was decisive in securing Arlova's execution (source: Darkness_at_Noon_9780226449616.md).
- **The Guilt**: In prison, Rubashov is haunted by her passive submission ("You will always be able to do what you like with me") and the physical memory of her body, realizing he must pay for his compromise (source: Darkness_at_Noon_9780226449616.md).
