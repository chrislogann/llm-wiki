# The Return of the Legend

**Summary**: An academic article exploring the resurgence of Anatoly Kashpirovsky's popularity during the COVID-19 pandemic, analyzing how global instability, medical distrust, and feelings of powerlessness led post-Soviet and immigrant communities to embrace his online health sessions.

**Sources**: `5-Voloshyna.md`

**Last updated**: 2026-08-18

---

## Overview
Written by Iryna Voloshyna of Indiana University, Bloomington, this article examines how alternative healer and psychotherapist [[anatoly-kashpirovsky]] utilized YouTube live streams during the 2020–2021 COVID-19 pandemic to offer virtual treatments (source: 5-Voloshyna.md). The study connects the revival of late-Soviet occultism and healing practices to systemic institutional failures, information gaps, and geopolitical anxieties (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).

## Scientific Legitimacy vs. Vernacular Belief
The article highlights a central tension in Kashpirovsky's persona:
* **The "Scientific" Claim**: Kashpirovsky insists his methods are medical and scientific, relying on the body's subconscious "biological self-regulatory" function—a concept closely aligned with Bonnie O'Connor's definition of *vitalism*, where the body possesses its own self-healing life force (source: 5-Voloshyna.md). He explicitly rejects terms like "hypnosis," "extrasensory," "folk healer," or "charlatan" (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **The Vernacular Practice**: Despite his claims, his audience treats him as a faith healer or magician. His patients engage in religious-like rituals, such as placing jars of water near their screens during YouTube broadcasts to replicate Orthodox Christian blessings (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **Telebridge Disclosures**: The success of his famous late-1980s TV surgeries without anesthesia has been re-evaluated. Patients later admitted they were in intense pain but stayed silent due to television pressure, and doctors actually administered local anesthesia (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).

## Geopolitical Vaccine Skepticism
Kashpirovsky tapped into anti-Western and Cold War-era conspiracy theories during the pandemic (source: 5-Voloshyna.md):
* **Anti-Western Rhetoric**: He expressed deep mistrust of Western or "Anglo-Saxon" vaccines, claiming the West was attempting to destroy Russia and drive its population to extinction (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **Russian Vaccine Distrust**: He also criticized the Russian vaccine, Sputnik V, using the controversial death of Yalta Mayor Ivan Imgrunt to suggest the vaccine itself caused the mayor's death, despite official denials (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).

## Experiential Source and Marginalization
Drawing on David Hufford’s *experiential source hypothesis* and Diane Goldstein's work on AIDS legends, the author argues that conspiracy thinking and alternative healing are not signs of ignorance, but rather structural coping mechanisms for marginalized populations (source: 5-Voloshyna.md):
* **Powerlessness**: Lockdowns and medical failures generated feelings of powerlessness, triggering the compensatory function of folklore (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
* **Marginalized Audiences**: Kashpirovsky's primary viewer base in 2020 consisted of post-Soviet citizens facing unstable healthcare networks, and émigrés in Brighton Beach, New York, who lacked health insurance and felt culturally isolated (source: 5-Voloshyna.md).
